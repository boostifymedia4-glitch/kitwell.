PNG is the format that screenshots, logos and many exported pictures arrive in. It keeps every pixel exactly, it can hold transparent areas, and it is very reliable. It also makes large files, especially for photographs. When a form asks for a JPG, when an email is too big or when a page loads slowly because of heavy pictures, the usual answer is to convert the PNG to a JPG.

The conversion is quick, but it is not free of side effects. JPG throws away some detail and cannot store transparency. This guide explains when converting is a good idea, what changes in the picture, how to handle transparent areas and how to get a good result.

:::takeaways
- JPG files are usually much smaller than PNG files for photographs, because JPG removes detail that is hard to see.
- JPG cannot store transparency, so transparent areas are filled with white.
- Screenshots, text and logos can show fuzzy edges in a JPG. Keep those as PNG when sharpness matters.
- The tool uses a fixed, high quality setting, so there is no slider to get wrong.
- Convert a copy and keep the original PNG, because a JPG cannot be turned back into the exact original.
:::

## PNG and JPG in a nutshell

PNG is lossless. When you save a picture as PNG, every pixel is stored exactly as it was, and opening and saving it again does not damage it. It supports transparency, so a logo can sit on any background. The price is size: the file stores a lot of information.

JPG is lossy. It divides the picture into small blocks and stores an approximation of each one, throwing away fine differences that the eye notices least. For photographs, with their smooth gradients and natural noise, the approximation is hard to tell from the original, and the file is a fraction of the size. Each time you re-save a JPG, a little more detail is lost.

## When converting PNG to JPG is a good idea

Convert when:

- The picture is a photograph, or looks like one. Photos in PNG are very large, and the JPG looks the same.
- A website, an application form or a marketplace accepts only JPG.
- You need to send many pictures by email and the total is too large.
- A page is slow because it carries heavy PNG photographs.
- You want a smaller file for storage and will not edit the picture further.

## When to keep the PNG

Do not convert when:

- The picture has transparent areas that you need, such as a logo on a coloured page.
- It is a screenshot, a diagram or any picture with sharp text and thin lines. JPG blurs the edges around letters, and you see faint speckles near them.
- You plan to edit the picture again. Editing a lossy file and saving it again adds more damage each time.
- It is a flat graphic with few colours. PNG compresses these well, and a JPG can end up bigger and uglier.

If you are unsure, convert a copy and compare the two at the size where the picture will be used.

## What happens to transparent areas

A JPG file has no place to store transparency. When a PNG with a transparent background is converted, those areas have to become some colour, and the tool fills them with white.

That works well when the picture will sit on a white page. It is a problem when the logo is meant for a dark or coloured background, because the white box around it will show. In that case, keep the PNG, or place the logo on the right background colour before you convert.

Check a converted picture on the background where it will be used. White fringes around a cut-out are a sign that the transparency was flattened.

## Why edges look fuzzy

JPG works on blocks of eight by eight pixels. Where a sharp edge crosses a block, such as the edge of a letter on a plain background, the approximation cannot be perfect, and tiny blotches appear near the edge. You rarely see them in photographs, where nothing is perfectly sharp, but you do see them in screenshots and logos.

A higher quality setting reduces the effect and makes the file bigger. The fixed quality in this tool is high, which keeps the fuzz faint, but it cannot remove it. If the picture is full of text, the better choices are to keep the PNG or to try WebP.

## Privacy and details

The converted file does not keep camera and location details, and embedded colour profiles are not preserved either. This is useful when you share a picture, and a small risk if exact colours matter, since some pictures change slightly on screens that depend on colour profiles. Animated pictures use only their first frame.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Converting a logo with a transparent background and then being surprised by a white box.
- Converting screenshots with text and accepting fuzzy letters.
- Deleting the original PNG after converting.
- Converting a JPG to PNG and expecting the lost detail to come back.
- Re-saving the same JPG many times.
- Making a file smaller by quality alone, without resizing the pixels first.

## A quick checklist

1. Decide whether the picture is a photo or a graphic with text.
2. Check for transparent areas and the background where the picture will be used.
3. Convert a copy and compare it at the final size.
4. Resize first, then compress, if the file is still too large.
5. Keep the original PNG.
