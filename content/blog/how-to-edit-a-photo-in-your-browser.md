Most photos need only a little help. A picture taken indoors is too dark, a snapshot from a phone is slightly crooked, a product photo has too much empty space around it, or a poster needs a line of text. For these jobs you do not need a large program with layers and brushes. A simple editor in your browser is quicker, and it keeps the picture on your device.

This guide shows how to make everyday edits with a live preview, in which order the edits are applied, how to export without losing quality and which other tools complete the job.

:::takeaways
- You can adjust colours, apply a filter, rotate, crop, sharpen, add a vignette and add text, with a preview that updates as you go.
- Your original file is never changed. The edited picture is created as a new download.
- Edits are applied at the full size of the picture, not at the preview size.
- PNG keeps every pixel. For JPG and WebP, use a quality of 90 or higher to keep photos looking the same.
- Location and camera details are not copied into the edited picture.
:::

## How to edit a photo

The editor works inside your browser tab, so your photo is not uploaded.

1. Open the tool and add a photo. It accepts one photo at a time, up to 25 MB and about 50 megapixels.
2. Use the tabs to adjust colours, apply a filter, rotate or crop, and add text. The preview updates as you go.
3. Choose the format and download your edited photo.

Because the preview is a smaller version for speed, the edits are applied again at the full size when you export. The result is as sharp as your original.

## The order of edits

The editor applies edits in a fixed order: rotate and crop first, then colour adjustments, then blur and sharpen, then the vignette and finally the text. Knowing the order helps you predict the result.

- Cropping comes first, so every later effect applies only to the part you keep. A vignette, for instance, darkens the edges of the cropped picture, not of the original.
- Text comes last, so it is never blurred or recoloured by the effects that came before.
- If you want a different order, for example to sharpen after adding text, export once, then open the result and make the second edit.

## Fixing exposure and colour

Most photos improve with small steps.

1. Start with brightness if the picture is too dark or too bright.
2. Add a little contrast if it looks flat, and lower it if it looks harsh.
3. Adjust saturation last. A small increase makes colours livelier, and too much makes skin look orange.
4. Look at the picture at full size and then at a small size, the way people will see it.

Make changes in small amounts. A photo that looks edited is usually over-edited. If you are unsure, take the sliders back to roughly half of where you first put them.

## Rotating and cropping

A crooked horizon or a wrong orientation is easy to fix. Rotate by a quarter turn for portrait or landscape corrections, and by a small angle to level a crooked picture. Then crop to remove empty space and to give the subject room.

Crop for a purpose. A square crop suits a profile picture, a wide crop suits a banner and a tall crop suits a story. ## Sharpening and blurring

Sharpening increases the contrast at edges, which makes a slightly soft picture look crisper. Use a small amount. Too much creates bright outlines around objects, which look artificial.

Blur softens a picture and can be used to calm a busy background. It affects the whole picture, so it is not the way to hide a face or a number. ## Adding text

Text is useful for labels, captions and posters. Choose a size that is readable at the size where the picture will be seen, and a colour that contrasts with the background behind it. White text on a bright sky is hard to read, and dark text on a dark corner disappears.

Because text is applied last, it always sits on top of every other edit and is not affected by the effects. ## Exporting without losing quality

The last step is the format, and it matters.

- PNG keeps every pixel. Choose it when you plan to edit the picture again or need transparency, and expect a bigger file.
- JPG and WebP are lossy, which means that they make the file smaller by throwing away detail that is hard to see. A quality of 90 or higher keeps photos looking the same as the original to most eyes.

Do not edit and save the same JPG again and again. Each save applies compression once more, and detail slowly disappears. Keep your original, make all edits in one session and export once. ## Common mistakes to avoid

- Over-editing, so that the picture looks unnatural.
- Saving a JPG again and again, which lowers quality each time.
- Cropping too tightly and leaving no room for later changes.
- Choosing a text colour that disappears on part of the picture.
- Forgetting to keep the original file.
- Judging the picture only at full size and not at the size where it will be seen.

## A quick checklist

1. Rotate and crop first.
2. Fix brightness and contrast, then saturation, in small steps.
3. Sharpen a little, if needed.
4. Add text last, with a colour that stands out.
5. Export once, at quality 90 or higher, and keep the original.
