You attach a PDF, press send and get a message that the file is too large. It always happens at the worst moment, such as a job application, a visa form or an invoice that has to go out today. The good news is that most PDFs can be made much smaller in a few minutes, and you rarely need special software.

This guide explains how email limits really work, why some PDFs are heavy and the order of steps that gives the best result with the least loss of quality.

:::takeaways
- Check the limit first. Many providers allow around 25 MB per email, and the limit counts everything attached, not only your PDF.
- Attachments grow by roughly a third when they are encoded for email, so a file close to the limit may still be refused.
- Pictures are the usual reason a PDF is heavy. Compressing them gives the biggest saving.
- Removing pages you do not need and splitting a long file are the next best steps.
- If the file is still too large, send a link instead of an attachment.
:::

## Know the limit before you start

Email limits differ and they change, so check the current rules of your provider and, if you can, of the person who will receive the message. As a common example, Gmail documents a limit of 25 MB for the total size of one message, and larger files are handled through a link. Other providers and company mail systems may allow less, and the receiving side can be stricter than the sending side.

Two details surprise people.

- The limit counts the whole message, so three attachments of 9 MB together are over a 25 MB limit.
- Attachments are encoded for transport, which makes them about a third larger. A file of 20 MB can therefore count as more than 25 MB.

A safe rule is to aim for well below the stated limit, for example 15 MB or less when the limit is 25 MB, and much smaller for strict systems such as application portals.

## Why a PDF is heavy

Text is small. A page of plain text takes only a few kilobytes. A PDF becomes heavy because of what is inside it.

- Photos and scans, which are stored as pictures. A scanned page is a full picture, so a 40-page scan can be larger than a 400-page text document.
- High-resolution pictures that are shown small on the page.
- Embedded fonts, which add up when a document uses many.
- Many pages, which multiply everything above.

Finding out which one applies tells you which fix will work. If the document is mostly text and still large, fonts or leftover data are likely. If it is mostly pictures, compress the pictures.

## Step 1: Compress the PDF

Start here, because it often solves the problem alone.

## Step 4: Fix the source when it is a scan

Scans are the worst offenders, and the best fix happens before the PDF exists. If you make the PDF from photos of paper pages, a few choices keep it small.

## When it is still too large

Some files cannot be shrunk enough without hurting quality, such as large plans or photo books. In that case, do not fight the limit.

- Use a link. Cloud storage services let you share a file by link, and most mail providers do this automatically for large attachments.
- Use a temporary share link. Kitwell tools that produce a file can create a share link that stays active for 24 hours, which is useful for a one-time transfer. Anyone with the link can download the file during that time, so share it only with the person you mean to send it to.
- Ask the recipient about their preferred method. Some organisations have a secure upload portal.

If the document is private, protect it before you send it. ## Check the result before you send

1. Open the smaller file and look at the first page, a page with pictures and the last page.
2. Zoom in on text and on any signature, stamp or small print.
3. Check the page count and the file name.
4. Check the total size of the message, including every attachment.
5. Keep the original file in case the recipient asks for a better version.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Compressing a file that has no pictures and expecting a big change.
- Compressing again and again, which loses quality with every round.
- Forgetting that the limit counts all attachments together.
- Aiming exactly at the limit instead of below it.
- Deleting the original after compressing.
- Sending a private file by link without a password or without checking who can open it.

:::faq Frequently asked questions
Q: How big can a PDF be for email?
A: It depends on the provider. Gmail, for example, documents 25 MB for a whole message, and other providers or company systems can allow less. The receiving side can also be stricter, so aim well below the limit.

Q: Why is my PDF still large after compressing?
A: Compression recompresses the JPEG pictures inside the file. If your PDF is mostly text, fonts or non-JPEG graphics, there is little to gain. Try removing pages or splitting the file.

Q: Will compression make my text blurry?
A: No. Text stays sharp because only the pictures are recompressed. Pictures can lose some detail, so check the pages afterwards.

Q: Is it safe to compress a private document online?
A: Compress PDF runs in your browser tab, so the file stays on your device and is not uploaded for compression.

Q: What can I do if the file cannot be made small enough?
A: Send a link instead of an attachment, split the document into parts or ask the recipient for their preferred method.
:::
