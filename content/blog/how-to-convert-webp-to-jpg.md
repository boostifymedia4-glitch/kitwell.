You save a picture from a website, try to attach it to a job application, and the form says the file type is not supported. You look at the file and find that it ends in .webp. Many sites now serve pictures in WebP because it makes pages load faster, so more and more downloads arrive in that format, yet plenty of programs and forms were built before WebP existed and still expect JPG.

This guide explains what WebP is, why conversion to JPG is sometimes needed, what happens to transparency and animation, and how to convert files in your browser in a few clicks.

:::takeaways
- WebP is a modern image format that makes smaller files; JPG is the format that is accepted almost everywhere.
- Converting WebP to JPG is a one-way trip for transparency: transparent areas are filled with white.
- Animated WebP files are converted using their first frame only.
- The tool re-encodes the picture at a high, fixed quality, so the result looks the same at normal sizes.
- Camera and location metadata are not kept in the converted file.
:::

## What is WebP, and why do you meet it so often?

WebP was created for the web. It can compress like JPG, with some loss of detail, or like PNG, without loss, and it supports transparency and animation. At similar visual quality, WebP files are typically 20 to 35 percent smaller than JPG, which is why browsers, content management systems and image services convert uploads to it automatically.

When you right-click and save a picture from a modern site, you may therefore get a WebP even though the original upload was a JPG. Nothing is wrong with the file; it simply is not the format your destination expects.

## Where WebP still causes problems

- Some upload forms, such as job portals and government sites, accept only JPG, PNG or PDF.
- Older email clients and office programs may not display WebP pictures.
- Image editors that are not updated can fail to open them.
- Some printing services only take JPG.
- Marketplaces and CMS plugins occasionally reject files by extension.

In all these cases a JPG copy solves the problem, because JPG is accepted nearly everywhere, from phones to photo labs.

## Transparency becomes white

JPG cannot store transparency. A WebP with a transparent background, such as a logo or a product cut-out, has to be flattened onto something. The tool fills the transparent areas with white. That looks right on a white page, but if you need the transparency to remain, convert to PNG instead with <tool_webp_to_png>WebP to PNG</tool_webp_to_png>, and use PNG wherever transparency matters.

If your logo ends up with a white box on a coloured page, that is the reason. Use the PNG version there.

## Animated WebP files

WebP can contain an animation, like a GIF. JPG cannot, so only the first frame is converted. If you need all frames, keep the WebP or use a tool designed for animation. For a still preview, the first-frame JPG is usually fine.

## Does conversion lose quality?

Converting between two lossy formats always involves another round of compression, so in theory some detail is lost. In practice, at a high quality setting, the difference is not visible at normal viewing sizes. To keep it minimal:

- Convert once, from the original WebP, not from an already converted JPG.
- Do not convert a picture back and forth between formats.
- Keep the WebP as your archive copy if the picture matters.

## Metadata and privacy

The converted file does not carry the original’s metadata. Camera model, date and GPS location are not included, and embedded colour profiles are not preserved. For sharing photos, this is often an advantage. The trade-off is that colours may shift slightly on some screens, particularly for pictures in wide-gamut colour spaces.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Converting a logo with a transparent background to JPG and then wondering about the white box.
- Converting a picture again and again, which compounds the loss.
- Renaming the extension from .webp to .jpg by hand. This does not convert anything and the file may still be rejected.
- Throwing away the original WebP.
- Expecting an animated picture to stay animated.

## A quick checklist

1. Check whether the picture has transparency or animation.
2. Use JPG for photographs and PNG when transparency matters.
3. Convert from the original WebP, once.
4. Compress or resize the JPG if there is a size limit.
5. Keep the WebP original.
