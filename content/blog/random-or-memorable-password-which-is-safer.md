Every account asks for a password, and everyone has the same dilemma. A long string of random characters is hard to guess but impossible to remember. Something you can remember is easier to use but may be easier to guess. So which one is safer, and which one should you actually use?

The honest answer is that it depends on where the password lives. This guide explains what makes a password strong, how random and memorable passwords differ and how to choose for each kind of account.

:::takeaways
- Uniqueness matters as much as strength. One password reused on many sites is the biggest everyday risk.
- A fully random password is the strongest for its length, and a password manager makes it practical.
- A memorable password is fine when it is long, built from unrelated words and not used anywhere else.
- Personal details, common words with simple swaps and patterns on a keyboard are weak, even if they look complicated.
- Turn on two-step verification wherever it is offered. It protects you even when a password leaks.
:::

## What makes a password strong

Attackers rarely type guesses by hand. Programs try huge numbers of possibilities, starting with the most likely: common passwords, names, dates, words with obvious changes, and passwords from earlier leaks. A strong password is one that does not appear on those lists and that would take too many tries to find by brute force.

Three things decide this.

1. Length. Each extra character multiplies the number of possibilities. A longer password is almost always better than a shorter one with more symbols.
2. Unpredictability. Characters or words chosen by chance are much harder to guess than anything a person invents, because people follow patterns.
3. Uniqueness. A password used on one site only cannot be reused against your other accounts when that site is breached.

A password that fails on any of these three is weak, however clever it looks.

## Fully random passwords

A random password is made by a program that picks every character by chance, such as a mix of letters, numbers and a few symbols. Its strength comes from having no pattern at all.

The strengths are clear.

- It has the maximum strength for its length.
- It carries no personal information and follows no habit.
- It is hard to guess even for someone who knows you well.

The weakness is just as clear: you cannot remember it. That is fine for most accounts, because a password manager remembers it for you. It is a poor choice for the few passwords you must type from memory, such as the one that unlocks the manager itself or your computer.

## Memorable passwords and passphrases

A memorable password is built so that a person can keep it in their head. The best way to do this is a passphrase: several unrelated words joined together, such as four or five words chosen by chance. Length does the work, because a long phrase has many possibilities even when every word is simple.

What makes a memorable password strong or weak is how it was chosen.

- Strong: words picked by chance, not connected to you or to each other, long enough, and used for one purpose only.
- Weak: a favourite song line, a pet name, a birth year, a keyboard row or a common word with the letter a replaced by an at sign. Attackers try exactly these patterns first.

## Which is safer

For the same length, a fully random password is the safer one, because it has no pattern for an attacker to use. A long passphrase can come close, and it has the advantage that you can remember and type it.

The real answer is that the style matters less than three habits.

- Never reuse a password. A very strong password that is used on ten sites is only as safe as the weakest of those ten sites.
- Make it long. Prefer length over complicated rules.
- Do not use personal information.

A medium-strength password that is unique and long beats a strong password that you reuse.

## Which to use for which account

A simple split works for most people.

- Most accounts, such as shops, forums and apps: a fully random password stored in a password manager.
- The master password of your manager, your device login and your email: a long passphrase that you can remember, used nowhere else.
- Accounts that you must type on a device where you cannot use your manager, such as a shared TV: a longer memorable password that is unique to that account.
- Files that you protect and share: a random password sent by a different channel from the file.

Your email account deserves special care, because most other accounts can be reset through it. Give it one of your strongest passwords and turn on two-step verification.

## Sharing a password safely

When you must share a password, do it in a way that limits the risk.

- Do not send the password in the same message as the file or the login name.
- Use a different channel for each, such as email for the file and a call or a chat for the password.
- Change it afterwards if it was a temporary one.

## Good habits that matter more than the style

1. Use a password manager for everything except the few passwords you must memorise.
2. Make each password unique.
3. Turn on two-step verification, especially for email, banking and social accounts.
4. Change a password at once if a service reports a breach, and do not change it on a schedule without a reason.
5. Never share a password through a public or unprotected channel.
6. Beware of messages that ask you to confirm a password. Go to the site directly instead of using a link.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Reusing one good password on many sites.
- Using a pet name, a birth year or a favourite phrase.
- Replacing letters with symbols in a common word and thinking it is safe.
- Writing the password in a plain note on your phone or a sticky note on your screen.
- Choosing a short password with many symbols instead of a long one.
- Sending the password together with the file it protects.
